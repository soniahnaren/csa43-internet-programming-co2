Question 5. Three hospitals and a diagnostic laboratory want to share patient records securely, give patients control over who can view their records, and keep a tamper-proof history of every access. Develop a design that integrates blockchain into the web services of the hospital network. Explain the role of blocks, hashes, consensus, smart contracts, and permissioned blockchains (for example, Hyperledger Fabric) compared with public blockchains; the decision to store only record hashes, consent and access logs on the blockchain while keeping the medical data off-chain in encrypted storage; and how REST web services connect the hospital web application to the blockchain and the database. Evaluate the design against confidentiality, integrity and availability, the conflict between immutability and the right to erasure under data-protection laws, scalability and cost, and key management, and recommend how these issues can be handled.

ANSWER 5 – Blockchain-integrated web services for sharing patient records

Idea. The hospitals and the laboratory need three things: (1) share records, (2) let the patient decide who may see them and (3) keep a history of every access that nobody can rewrite. A permissioned blockchain gives the shared, tamper-evident history. Medical data itself is not put on the chain: it stays in encrypted storage owned by the organisations, and the chain keeps only fingerprints (hashes), consent decisions and access events.

A. Blockchain concepts used

ConceptMeaningUse in this design
BlockA batch of transactions with a timestamp, the hash of the previous block and its own hashEach registered record, consent change and access event is a transaction stored in a block
HashA fixed-length fingerprint of data (SHA-256); any change to the data gives a completely different value and it cannot be reversedLinks blocks (changing an old block breaks every later link) and fingerprints each record so alteration of the stored file is detected
ConsensusThe rule by which the network agrees on which transactions are valid and in what orderTransactions must be endorsed by at least 2 of the 4 member organisations; an ordering service then puts them in a block; there is no mining
Smart contract (chaincode)Program that runs on the ledger and enforces rules automaticallyOnly a patient can grant or revoke consent for their own records; only clinicians register records; every access attempt (allowed or denied) is logged
Digital signatureProof that a message came from a key holderEach organisation signs the transactions it endorses (ed25519 in the model)

B. Permissioned compared with public blockchains

AspectPermissioned (Hyperledger Fabric)Public (Bitcoin, Ethereum)
Who can joinOnly identified, approved organisations (membership service, certificates)Anyone, anonymously
ConsensusEndorsement policy + ordering service (for example Raft); fast finality, no mining or gasProof-of-work or proof-of-stake; slower, confirmations needed
PerformanceHundreds to thousands of transactions per second are typical in tuned deploymentsLow throughput and variable delay
CostInfrastructure and operating cost of the consortium; no per-transaction feeFees (gas) paid per transaction
PrivacyChannels and private data collections restrict who sees data; identities are knownAll data is visible to everyone; pseudonymous addresses
Governance and complianceThe consortium controls membership, upgrades and who is liable; can follow health-data lawNo central party to hold responsible; hard to meet legal duties
Trust modelParticipants trust each other only partly and verify through the ledgerTrust in the open network and its economics

For hospitals that are legally responsible for patient data, a permissioned network is the right choice: known members, fast and free transactions, and control over visibility. A public chain would expose metadata to everyone and its costs and delays do not suit clinical work.

C. What goes on the chain and what stays off-chain

DataWhereWhy
Medical content (diagnosis, reports, images)Off-chain, encrypted (AES-256-GCM) in each organisation's storeLarge, sensitive, must be correctable and erasable; a blockchain is permanent and replicated to all peers
SHA-256 hash of the encrypted recordOn-chainProves the stored file has not been altered; reveals nothing because it is the hash of random-looking ciphertext
Consent (patient, organisation, purpose, expiry, revoked)On-chainShared, enforced the same way by every hospital, and its history cannot be rewritten
Access log (who, which record, purpose, allowed or denied, time)On-chainTamper-proof history; the patient can inspect it
Patient name, contact details, clinical textNever on-chainCannot be deleted later; every peer would hold it

Envelope encryption. Each record gets its own random data key (DEK). The record is encrypted with the DEK using AES-256-GCM (which also detects tampering). The DEK is stored only in a wrapped (encrypted) form under a master key (KEK) that is held in a key-management service or hardware security module. The hash that goes on the chain is the hash of the encrypted package.

D. REST web services between the web application, the database and the blockchain

Method and URIWhoWhat happens
POST /v1/recordsHospital or labEncrypts and stores the record off-chain; registers its hash on the chain; returns 201, recordId, hash and block number
GET /v1/records/{id}Hospital or labChecks consent on the chain; records the access attempt; verifies the stored data against the on-chain hash; decrypts and returns it (or 403 / 409 / 410)
POST /v1/patients/{id}/consentsThe patient onlyGrants an organisation access for a purpose and a number of days (a chain transaction)
DELETE /v1/patients/{id}/consents/{org}The patient onlyRevokes consent (a chain transaction, 204)
GET /v1/records/{id}/auditPatient or auditorReturns every access event for the record from the ledger
DELETE /v1/records/{id}/contentPatient or owner organisationRight-to-erasure request: destroys the key and data (crypto-shredding), logs that erasure occurred
GET /v1/ledger/verifyAny memberRecomputes all hashes and links; reports the first broken block
  1. Reading a record: the web app calls GET /v1/records/{id} with the user's token.
  2. The service reads the record entry from the ledger and asks the contract whether the caller's organisation owns the record or holds an active consent from the patient.
  3. It writes an ACCESS transaction (allowed or denied) that the peers endorse and add to a block.
  4. If denied, it returns 403; the denied attempt remains visible to the patient.
  5. If allowed, it compares the hash of the stored package with the on-chain hash (mismatch means tampering: 409), then decrypts it and returns the content.

E. Evaluation against confidentiality, integrity and availability

PropertyHow the design supports itWeaknesses and remedies
ConfidentialityNo medical data on the chain; AES-256-GCM encryption off-chain; consent enforced by the contract before any decryption; role checks; TLS between all components; Fabric channels or private data collections to limit who sees metadataMetadata (who accessed what, when) is visible to members: use pseudonymous patient IDs and channels per consortium purpose. A compromised web service could read plaintext: use least privilege, separate service accounts and an HSM
IntegrityHash chain makes block edits detectable; endorsements from several organisations; hash of each encrypted record on the ledger; GCM authentication tag; signatures identify who actedTested: an edited block was detected, and altered off-chain data returned 409. Remaining risk: wrong data entered at the source ("garbage in, immutable out"): validate at entry and allow signed corrections that reference the original
AvailabilityEvery organisation runs peers and keeps a copy of the ledger; ordering service with several nodes (Raft); off-chain stores can be replicated and backed upIf fewer than the required organisations are online, new transactions stop (endorsement policy of 2 of 4 tolerates two being offline). Emergency "break-glass" access needs a documented fallback that is logged afterwards. Plan backups, monitoring and recovery drills

F. Immutability against the right to erasure

The conflict. A blockchain is designed so that history cannot be changed, but data-protection laws (for example GDPR Article 17, India's DPDP Act 2023 and similar rules) give people rights to correction and erasure. If personal data were written on the chain, erasure would be impossible.

  • Do not store personal data on-chain (the decision in section C). The chain holds only hashes, pseudonymous identifiers, consent and access events.
  • Crypto-shredding: to erase a record, destroy its data key and the encrypted file. The ciphertext can no longer be decrypted and the remaining hash cannot be linked to readable data. The test shows the record then returns 410 Gone while the ledger still verifies.
  • Hash design: hash the *encrypted* package (random nonce, so equal records give different hashes) rather than the plain text, so that dictionary guessing is not possible.
  • Corrections: write a new record version and register its hash; the old version is marked superseded or erased off-chain.
  • Open legal point: whether a hash can still count as personal data is debated and depends on the jurisdiction; the design reduces the risk (no readable data, key destroyed) but a legal review and a data-protection impact assessment are required. Medical-record retention laws may also require keeping records for a period, so erasure requests must be checked against them.

G. Scalability, cost and key management

IssueProblemRecommendation
ScalabilityEvery access is a ledger transaction; endorsement and ordering add delay; the ledger grows foreverKeep payloads tiny (hash plus identifiers); store data off-chain; batch low-risk events; use several channels per purpose; tune block size and timeouts; archive old blocks and keep world-state small; read-only checks (consent lookup) can be queries that do not need ordering
CostRunning peers, ordering nodes, certificate authority, monitoring and skilled staff for 4 organisations; no per-transaction fee but real operating costShare the ordering service in the consortium; use managed blockchain services if allowed; start with a pilot on consent and audit only; compare with a conventional append-only audit database to justify the added trust
Key managementLost keys lock out data; stolen keys expose it; many key types (organisation identities, patient identities, data keys, master key)Organisation keys in an HSM and certificates from the Fabric CA with rotation and revocation; patient identity via the portal login with strong authentication (patients do not manage raw private keys; the portal signs on their behalf after verification); per-record DEKs wrapped by a KEK in a KMS, with KEK rotation; separation of duties; backup and recovery of keys; documented break-glass procedure; audit all key use
Operations and governanceChaincode bugs are hard to correct; membership changes; disputesTest and audit chaincode, versioned upgrades approved by the members, written consortium rules and an agreed process to add or remove a hospital

H. Recommendation

Use a permissioned Hyperledger Fabric network run by the three hospitals and the laboratory, with an endorsement policy of at least two organisations. Put only record hashes, consent and access events on the chain, keep encrypted medical data off-chain with per-record keys, and expose everything to the web application through authenticated REST services that check consent on the ledger, verify the hash and log every attempt. Handle erasure by crypto-shredding, protect keys with an HSM/KMS, and limit throughput and cost by keeping transactions small. Before go-live carry out a legal review and a data-protection impact assessment, run the chaincode through security testing and define break-glass access for emergencies. The Node.js code supplied is a teaching model of these ideas; the Fabric chaincode file is a reference sample that was not run on a Fabric network.
